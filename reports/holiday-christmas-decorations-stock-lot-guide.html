Christmas & Holiday Decorations Stock Lots — the B2B Buying Guide

What China's holiday décor surplus actually looks like on the clearance market — where it comes from, indicative price bands by category, the season clock that decides pricing power, the licensed-character IP trap, and the electrical and packing checks that protect fragile lots.

Key takeaways.

  • Holiday décor runs on a hard surplus clock: Christmas goods turn surplus within weeks after late December, Halloween goods after October 31 — residue is most abundant in the 8–10 weeks following each season.
  • Indicative bands: ornaments typically USD 0.05–0.5/pc, garlands and wreaths USD 0.3–2/pc, string lights USD 0.5–3/set, artificial trees USD 3–20/pc, inflatables USD 5–20/pc.
  • Licensed characters are the biggest IP trap: undocumented recognizable character print should be skipped, not discounted; generic seasonal artwork (Santa, snowman, reindeer) is usually unowned.
  • Lights and inflatables are electrical goods: voltage/plug matching, waterproof rating and CE/RoHS-style documentation must be verified before payment; lithium-battery items may need UN38.3 for shipping.
  • Fragile and mixed-SKU packing decides value: ornaments and glass goods need packing-density and drop-test evidence, and décor lots routinely mix many SKUs — count against the packing list, not photos.

1. Why holiday decoration surplus exists at all

China's décor belt (Yiwu for ornaments, garlands and party goods; Zhejiang and Guangdong for lighting and inflatables) produces the world's seasonal decorations months ahead of each holiday. Because demand is date-locked, leftover stock has no second season at full price — it flows into the clearance market through five doors:

  • Post-season retailer residue. Retailers and wholesalers stop ordering the moment the date passes; whatever sits in distribution centres after the season is cleared in bulk lots. Christmas décor residue typically peaks late December through February.
  • Cancelled export orders. A container of finished decorations that misses its sailing window loses its buyer — the goods are date-locked but the products themselves stay sellable to any destination market next season.
  • Packaging refreshes. When a retail chain updates artwork or private-label branding, old-packaging stock is liquidated wholesale even though the product is unchanged.
  • Factory overproduction. Factories routinely run extra units to hedge against mid-season quality rejections; the hedge tail becomes surplus once the order ships.
  • Commercial and theme installations. Malls, hotels and event companies replace oversized trees, figures and light installations on refresh cycles; decommissioned commercial décor enters the market as heavy-duty one-off lots.

3. The season clock: counter-season vs in-season buying

Holiday décor is the purest counter-season category in the stock-lot market. The table below summarizes the typical trade-off:

Counter-season (buy after the season)In-season (buy before the season)
Typical windowChristmas: late Dec–Feb; Halloween: Oct–NovSep–Dec for Christmas; Aug–Oct for Halloween
Typical pricingDeepest discounts — sellers hold dead stock with carrying costsCloser to new-production wholesale; stock thins fast
Choice of lotsWidest — residue from many suppliers at onceNarrow — the good residue has already moved
Aging riskHigher — goods sat in warehouse for months; check compression, fading, battery corrosionLower — lots are fresher

The practical pattern for small importers: buy residue counter-season at clearance pricing, warehouse it, and enter the next season with landed costs below the in-season competition. Storage cost and the patience to hold inventory for months are the price of that margin.

4. The licensed-character IP trap

Décor is where licensed-character risk bites hardest, because seasonal print runs to recognizable characters: film and cartoon figures, sports mascots, branded Christmas imagery. The line is simple:

  • Generic seasonal artwork — Santa, snowmen, reindeer, stars, generic nativity scenes — is typically unowned and safe to trade.
  • Recognizable character artwork requires documented authorization from the rights holder. If the seller cannot show a licence matched to the actual goods, skip the SKU — this is a skip, not a discount.
  • Branded stock (retail-chain private label, brand-collaboration décor) trades under trademark-exhaustion practice: the trademark belongs to its owner, goods are not authorized new production, and re-labelling or repackaging to obscure origin is off-limits.

5. Electrical and battery compliance for lights and inflatables

String lights, pre-lit trees, motif lights and inflatables with blowers are electrical goods and carry the same checks as small appliances:

  • confirm rated voltage and plug format against your destination market (220–240V EU/UK vs 110–120V US) before paying — surplus sellers rarely volunteer which market a lot was built for;
  • check the waterproof rating (IP-marked) on outdoor-rated light lines, and ask for CE/RoHS-style documentation matched to the actual lot photos, not the product family;
  • battery-operated items containing lithium coin cells or battery packs may require UN38.3 test summaries for transport — ask before booking freight, because carriers enforce the lithium line strictly;
  • sample-run blower motors on inflatables and check controllers and light sequences on pre-lit trees — motor and controller failures are common warehouse-aging failures.

6. Grading décor stock: the fragile-packing checks

Grading follows the A/B/mixed convention used across the stock-lot market, with four category-specific checks:

  • Breakage exposure. Glass ornaments and ceramic figurines are only as good as their packing — ask for packing-density details and inner-box photos, and weigh breakage allowances into your offer on glass lines.
  • Compression and fading. Garlands, wreaths and fabric goods deform under stacked storage; check for permanent flattening and colour shift on sun-exposed carton faces.
  • Set completeness. Themed kits (advent sets, table-settings, multi-piece prop kits) routinely ship missing pieces — count against the packing list, not photos.
  • Battery-compartment corrosion. Battery-operated décor that sat in humid warehouses grows corroded contacts; sample-open battery doors on a cross-section of SKUs.

7. How to buy: five-step checklist

Step 1 — Time the purchase counter-season

Buy Christmas residue late December through February and Halloween residue October through November. That's when lot choice is widest and sellers' carrying costs push prices to the floor. Verify the artwork is not date-locked beyond the next season (dated packaging, year prints).

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • full carton photos including inner packing and packaging variants;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card;
  • electrical documentation for light and inflatable lines (voltage, plug, CE/RoHS-style papers).

Step 3 — Screen the print before the price

Sort SKUs into generic artwork (buy), documented licensed (buy with the licence on file), and undocumented licensed (skip). Do the sort before negotiating — a lot priced 30% below market with unlicensed character print is not a bargain.

Step 4 — Verify the electrical SKUs

Match voltage and plug to your destination market, check waterproof ratings on outdoor lines, and ask about lithium cells for battery goods. Skip electrical SKUs the seller cannot document — light-string failures generate returns that erase the clearance discount.

Step 5 — Mix your container

Décor is light-to-medium cargo and consolidates cleanly with household, toy and textile stock in one mixed load — keep heavy commercial décor and pre-lit trees as dedicated pallet lines. Mixed loading is standard practice in Yiwu and typically the biggest landed-cost lever for small importers.

Frequently asked questions

Where do Christmas and holiday decoration stock lots come from?

The main doors are post-season retailer and wholesaler residue (typically cleared within weeks after the season), cancelled export orders, packaging refreshes that strand old-artwork goods, factory overproduction hedges, and decommissioned commercial décor from mall, hotel and event installations.

What do holiday decoration stock lots typically cost?

Indicative clearance ranges are roughly USD 0.05–0.5/pc for ornaments, USD 0.3–2/pc for garlands and wreaths, USD 0.5–3/set for string lights, USD 3–20/pc for artificial trees, USD 5–20/pc for inflatables and USD 0.2–1.5/pc for Halloween props. Actual prices depend on quantity, condition, season timing and market.

When is the best time to buy?

Counter-season: Christmas décor residue is most abundant late December through February, Halloween goods October through November. Counter-season buying typically wins the deepest discounts and the widest choice, at the cost of warehouse storage until the next season.

What IP risks come with holiday décor lots?

Licensed characters are the biggest trap — undocumented recognizable character print should be skipped entirely. Generic seasonal artwork is typically unowned. Branded and private-label stock trades under trademark-exhaustion practice with no re-labelling or repackaging.

What compliance checks matter for lights and inflatables?

Voltage and plug matching for the destination market, waterproof ratings on outdoor lines, CE/RoHS-style documentation matched to the actual goods, and UN38.3 summaries where lithium cells are present. Sample-run inflatable blower motors and light controllers — they are common aging failures.
