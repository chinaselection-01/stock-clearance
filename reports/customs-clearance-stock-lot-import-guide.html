Customs Clearance for Stock Lots — the B2B Import & Logistics Guide

How experienced importers move surplus and clearance cargo through customs without surprises: HS classification for mixed lots, declaring the real transaction value, the documentation pack, destination-market entry requirements, duty math and the Incoterms that fit one-off stock deals.

Key takeaways.

  • Classify per line, not per container: each SKU group gets its own HS code, quantity and value line on the declaration; a single generic code for a mixed container is a classic cause of customs holds.
  • Declare the real transaction price. Clearance prices are legitimate transaction values; under-invoicing is where importers actually get penalised.
  • Regulated goods stay regulated in stock form: CE/UKCA, food-contact declarations and lithium-battery documents (UN38.3, MSDS) are checked on arrival regardless of how cheap the lot was.
  • Mixed-container deals run cleanest with one packing list per supplier, so each origin and value segment is traceable to its own declaration lines.
  • FOB is the workhorse Incoterm for stock deals — the buyer controls destination clearance; full DDP from a Chinese seller is the riskiest structure.

1. Why clearance cargo gets extra scrutiny

Stock lots are not risky at customs because they are cheap. They are risky because of how they travel:

  • Many SKUs, one container. A mixed clearance container can hold dozens of product types. Every one of them must be classified, valued and declared — sloppiness shows up immediately.
  • Low invoice values. Deep discounts sit far below customs reference prices for comparable goods, which attracts valuation queries. The answer is documentation, not inflated values.
  • Regulated goods in disguise. Toys, electricals, food-contact plastics and battery-powered items carry the same compliance duties as first-run goods, no matter what they cost.
  • One-off shipments. A first-time importer with a single clearance shipment and no history gets the conservative treatment from most customs regimes.

None of this is a reason to avoid stock lots — it is a reason to prepare the file before the goods ship, not after they are held.

2. HS classification for mixed and multi-SKU lots

The governing principle: classify per line, not per container. Each product group on the packing list maps to its own HS (harmonized system) code, with its own quantity, unit and value. Practical pattern:

Packing-list groupDeclaration treatmentTypical failure mode
One product family per carton range (e.g. mugs, then storage boxes)Separate tariff lines, HS by material and functionNone — the clean case
Assorted goods sold "as one lot"Still split into tariff lines by what the goods are; use the seller's packing list and photos to build linesDeclaring one generic code for everything — reassessment and penalties on audit
Goods-of-set (e.g. a grooming kit with case)Check set-classification rules in the destination tariff (sets classified by essential character)Splitting a true set into components and over/under-paying duty
Textiles, footwear, electronicsHigh-attention chapters in most tariffs — quota, licensing or higher dutyMis-stating material composition (e.g. calling synthetic knitwear "cotton")

A mixed household container typically lands between several and a few dozen tariff lines. A good customs broker can build the lines from the packing list; what the broker cannot fix is a packing list that does not match the cargo.

3. Valuation: declare what you actually paid

Customs valuation in most markets is based on the price paid or payable for the goods. A clearance price is a genuine transaction price — surplus lots are worth less because they are surplus, and that is not a violation. What is:

  • Under-invoicing — declaring less than actually paid to save duty. Customs catches this with reference prices, physical inspection and post-clearance audit; penalties plus recovery cost multiples of the duty "saved".
  • Split payments — paying part of the price off-invoice. This breaks the valuation chain and is treated as under-invoicing in most regimes.
  • Value that can't be evidenced — keep the proforma invoice, the final invoice and proof of payment consistent. On stock deals where price was negotiated on WhatsApp, write the agreed price into the invoice; the chat record is supporting evidence, not a substitute.

If a valuation query comes, the winning file is simple: invoice = contract = payment = packing list, all telling the same story.

6. Duty and tax math on a clearance lot

Duty is charged on the declared customs value (typically the transaction price, plus freight and insurance where the valuation basis requires it — CIF-based bases in many markets, FOB-based in others such as the US). The stack that lands on a typical EU import, using indicative round numbers:

  • Customs duty: product-specific, commonly low single digits to low double digits for consumer goods lines.
  • Import VAT: charged on customs value + duty + freight (EU example: typically 19–25% depending on member state).
  • Broker and port charges: fixed-entry fees plus per-line charges — which is why dozens of tariff lines on a mixed container cost more to clear than a single-SKU shipment.

The planning consequence for stock buyers: the clearance price advantage survives duty in most categories, but thin-margin categories (low-value textiles, some plasticware) feel both the duty rate and the multi-line entry fees hardest. Run the landed-cost math per line before committing, not after arrival.

On consolidated mixed containers, pair the Incoterm choice with the document pattern from our mixed container loading guide: one master B/L, one packing list per supplier, and declaration lines that follow those lists.

8. The five-step pre-shipment clearance checklist

Step 1 — Fix the tariff lines before booking

Send the packing list to your broker and pre-classify. Confirm duty rates per line and decide whether a preferential origin document applies.

Step 2 — Align invoice, contract and payment

One price, written everywhere. No off-invoice components.

Step 3 — Assemble the compliance file for regulated SKUs

Identify which lines are regulated (electricals, toys, food-contact, batteries) and collect certificates while the goods are still in the seller's warehouse — after loading is too late.

Step 4 — Choose the Incoterm that matches your clearance capability

FOB with your own broker where possible; avoid DDP from unknown sellers.

Step 5 — File pre-alerts on time

ISF in the US before loading, entry pre-data in other regimes per local rules. Late filings are among the cheapest problems to prevent and among the most annoying to fix.

Frequently asked questions

How do you classify HS codes for a mixed stock lot?

Per line, not per container: each SKU group gets its own HS code, quantity and value on the declaration. A mixed household container typically splits into several to a few dozen tariff lines. One generic code for the whole container is a classic cause of holds and penalty reassessments.

What value should I declare for clearance goods?

The real transaction price you paid. Deep clearance discounts are legitimate transaction values; the violation that gets importers penalised is under-invoicing — declaring less than actually paid — which customs detects through reference prices, inspection and audit.

What documents are needed to import a stock lot from China?

Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin or preferential form where applicable, plus product certificates for regulated goods (CE/UKCA reports, food-contact declarations, MSDS and UN38.3 for lithium batteries). Keep one packing list per supplier on consolidated containers.

Who is the importer of record for clearance goods?

A registered entity in the destination market — EORI in the EU, a customs bond and importer number in the US, and equivalents elsewhere. Buyers not established locally typically work through a customs broker or fiscal representative arranged before shipment.

Which Incoterm works best for stock-lot deals?

FOB at the Chinese port is the default: the buyer controls freight and destination clearance. CIF works for small parcels. Full DDP from a Chinese seller is the riskiest structure — destination duties and tax handled informally can strand goods and mask under-valuation.
