Seasonal Stock Lots — the B2B Peak-Season Buying Calendar

The month-by-month clock of surplus seasonal goods from China — when Christmas, back-to-school and summer stock lots peak, why counter-season buying typically wins the deepest discounts, and a five-step checklist for importers and discount retailers.

Key takeaways.

  • Seasonal surplus follows a predictable clock: lots typically surface in the weeks after each retail season ends — Christmas décor from late December through February, back-to-school stationery around July–September, summer goods from September onward.
  • Counter-season buying typically secures the deepest discounts, because sellers are shedding warehouse space, not chasing demand — off-season cuts can exceed the typical 30–70% stock-lot discount band.
  • Christmas stock should be booked by early autumn to land before peak retail; the counter-season (spring) window offers the widest choice at the lowest prices, at the cost of longer inventory carry.
  • Seasonal goods rarely expire, but exceptions matter: batteries, adhesives, inflatables and licensed characters age — always ask the date of manufacture.
  • Mixed loading is the norm: seasonal lots ship alongside evergreen surplus (household, drinkware, toys) in consolidated containers from 2–5 suppliers.

1. Why seasonal surplus is the most predictable stock-lot category

Most stock lots appear when something goes wrong — an order is cancelled, a buyer defaults, a forecast misses. Seasonal surplus is different: it appears on schedule, every year, because the retail calendar forces it. Three mechanisms guarantee the flow:

  • Season-end liquidation. Retail chains and importers return unsold seasonal goods to suppliers or refuse delivery; the factory must clear them at any price to make room for next season's production.
  • Pre-holiday overproduction. Factories produce ahead of orders for peak seasons (Christmas, Children's Day, back-to-school) to guarantee delivery capacity — when demand lands softer than forecast, the tail becomes surplus.
  • Warehouse-pressure sales. Before Chinese New Year and the summer factory-holiday season, storage gets tight; aged seasonal stock is sold off cheaply simply to empty racks.

For buyers this means seasonal stock lots can be planned around, not just stumbled upon — the calendar below is the planning tool.

Most experienced surplus buyers run a blend: one counter-season container for the price win, topped up in-season with whatever fast-moving lots appear.

4. What to check before buying seasonal stock lots

Step 1 — Date of manufacture first

Ask when the goods were produced. Hard décor stores well, but batteries corrode, adhesives dry out, inflatables and plush suffer in humid warehouses, and AA batteries included in electronic seasonal items may need replacement before resale. A lot two seasons old is not automatically bad — but it should be priced like it.

Step 2 — Check the licence, not just the goods

Licensed-character seasonal goods (film, cartoon, sports IPs) carry a resale-value clock: if the licence lapses or the property cools in your market, discount depth can't save the line. Branded and licensed goods in the secondary market are surplus stock, not authorised first-run retail goods — trademarks belong to their owners; confirm your market's resale rules before importing.

Step 3 — Demand the three proofs

  • carton photos including inner packing and assortment mix;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card;
  • assorted counts per carton (seasonal lots are frequently pre-packed assortments — verify pcs/carton and carton dimensions for your freight math).

Step 4 — Book inspection for big lots

Third-party inspection in the supplier's city typically costs USD 100–200 per man-day. For seasonal lots the two most common findings are wrong assortment counts and moisture-damaged cartons — both are cheap to catch before payment and expensive after.

Step 5 — Mix the container, plan the landing

Combine seasonal lots with evergreen surplus to fill a container, and work backwards from your shelf date: sea freight to Europe or North America typically runs several weeks, plus customs and distribution. If your selling season starts in November, cargo booked by early autumn is the usual rule of thumb.

Frequently asked questions

When is the best time to buy seasonal stock lots from China?

Surplus peaks in the weeks after each retail season ends: Christmas décor typically floods the channel from late December through February, back-to-school stationery around July–September, summer goods from September onward. Counter-season buying typically secures the deepest discounts because storage pressure, not demand, sets the price.

Why is counter-season buying usually cheaper?

Sellers clear warehouses before factory holidays and new-season production. An off-season buyer removes goods the seller would otherwise store for months, so discounts often run deeper than the typical 30–70% stock-lot band. In-season buyers compete with each other; counter-season buyers compete only with warehouse space.

How early must I buy Christmas stock lots to sell in season?

Work backwards from your shelf date: sea freight from China to Europe or North America typically takes several weeks, plus customs and distribution. Cargo should be booked by early autumn to land before peak retail. Counter-season (spring) buying gives the widest choice and lowest prices at the cost of longer inventory carry.

Do seasonal goods expire or degrade in storage?

Most hard-goods décor stores for years. Exceptions: batteries and adhesives age, inflatables and plush can suffer in humid storage, and licensed-character goods lose value if the licence lapses in your market. Always ask the date of manufacture and inspect storage conditions.

Can I mix seasonal and non-seasonal lots in one container?

Yes — mixed containers are standard in the Yiwu stock-lot trade. A typical load combines seasonal and evergreen surplus from 2–5 suppliers, one master bill of lading and a separate packing list per supplier.
