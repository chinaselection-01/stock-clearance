Tableware & Kitchenware Stock Lots — the B2B Pricing Guide

Where surplus dinner sets, mugs, cutlery, glassware and cookware come from, indicative price ranges by category, the food-contact compliance line, and the packing checks that decide whether a ceramic lot survives the voyage.

Key takeaways.

  • Tableware surplus is pattern-driven: discontinued decor, holiday leftovers and rebranding changes — not quality failures — push finished goods into the secondary market.
  • Indicative clearance bands: mugs typically USD 0.15–0.6/pc, ceramic dinner sets USD 3–15/set, glass tumblers USD 0.1–0.5/pc, stainless cutlery sets USD 0.5–2.5/set, cookware USD 2–8/pc.
  • Food-contact rules apply to stock goods exactly as to first-run goods: EU 1935/2004 (plus EU 84/500/EC for ceramic lead/cadmium), FDA in the US, LFGB in Germany — original test reports often come with the lot.
  • Breakage is the hidden cost: display-packed cartons with air padding re-ship badly; always ask for packing spec and a drop-test video before paying.
  • Counter-season buying works here: Christmas tableware is cheapest in January–March, Ramadan and gift-set ranges in the weeks after their peak — stock sellers clear warehouses once the window closes.

1. Why finished tableware ends up as stock lots

Ceramic and metal kitchenware is produced in long, decorated runs — and decoration is what strands it. Four recurring generators:

  • Discontinued patterns and colour obsolescence. Retailers refresh tableware ranges yearly; last season's decor is unsellable at full price but perfectly sellable in discount channels.
  • Holiday and gift-set leftovers. Christmas ranges, Ramadan sets and promotional gift boxes are made against a fixed selling window. When the window closes, unsold finished goods move to the stock market within weeks.
  • Cancelled or unclaimed export orders. A fired, glazed and packed container that misses its sailing window becomes surplus at a fraction of cost.
  • Packaging and branding changes. A retailer switching box design or logo strands the old run in the factory warehouse — the goods themselves are first-run quality.

The main supply belts are Chaozhou and Dehua (ceramics), Zibo in Shandong (ceramics), Jieyang (stainless cutlery) and Yongkang (metal kitchenware and cookware). For importers this means below-wholesale buying is structurally normal in decorated tableware.

3. Grading and inspecting tableware stock

A/B/mixed grading applies, but the inspection focus is material-specific:

  • Grade A: clean cartons, uniform decor, no glaze crazing or chips on sampled pieces, gift boxes uncrushed, piece counts match the box label.
  • Grade B: minor glaze marks, colour variation between cartons, crushed or water-marked gift boxes, or discontinued packaging — typically 20–40% below the A price for the same style.
  • Mixed lots: multi-pattern, multi-SKU pallets priced per carton or per kilo. Standard for mugs and tumblers; acceptable for dinner sets if the manifest lists patterns and piece counts.

For ceramic and glass specifically, ask the supplier for the carton weight, packing spec, and a random drop-test video of a packed carton. Display-packed goods (air padding, no dividers) were packed for retail shelves, not for re-shipping — if repacking is needed, that labour and material cost belongs in your landed-cost math, not the supplier's.

4. Food-contact compliance: stock does not mean exempt

Stock tableware sold into food-contact use carries the same regulatory line as first-run goods:

  • EU: Regulation (EC) 1935/2004 framework, plus EU 84/500/EC lead and cadmium migration limits for ceramic articles.
  • US: FDA food-contact requirements for the relevant materials.
  • Germany: LFGB as the stricter de-facto standard for kitchen contact goods.

The good news: lots that originated as EU or US export orders frequently already hold test reports from the original order. Always ask for them — a lot with existing food-contact documentation is worth a premium over an undocumented lot, because you avoid paying for fresh testing on aged or unlabelled stock.

5. How to buy: a five-step checklist

Step 1 — Price the packing, not just the product

Ask for carton dimensions, gross weight and packing spec before negotiating. A cheap-per-piece lot that repacks badly can lose its margin in breakage and repack labour.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • date of production and storage conditions (boxed ceramics absorb damp; gift boxes mould);
  • full carton photos including gift-box corners and inner dividers;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card.

Step 3 — Sample-test the material

For pallet-plus orders, third-party inspection in Chaozhou/Jieyang costs roughly USD 100–200 per man-day and typically pays for itself — glaze crazing, handle cracks and missing lid counts are the most common findings in tableware stock.

Step 4 — Negotiate on the lot

Stock sellers clear warehouses. "I take the whole lot, ex-warehouse, this week" gets a better price than per-piece haggling — especially in the weeks right after a holiday window closes.

Step 5 — Mix your container

Heavy ceramics pair badly with nothing — they are natural container ballast. Consolidate with apparel, toys or household goods from 2–3 suppliers in one 40ft load; heavy goods at the bottom also stabilise the whole mixed container.

Frequently asked questions

Where do tableware and kitchenware stock lots come from?

Most surplus tableware stock comes from cancelled export orders, discontinued decor patterns, holiday and gift-set leftovers after their selling window, retailer packaging or rebranding changes, and trade-fair samples. Chaozhou, Dehua and Zibo (ceramics), Jieyang (stainless cutlery) and Yongkang (metal kitchenware) are the main supply belts.

What do tableware stock lots typically cost?

Indicative FOB-style clearance ranges: mugs typically USD 0.15–0.6/pc, ceramic dinner sets USD 3–15/set, glass tumblers USD 0.1–0.5/pc, stainless cutlery sets USD 0.5–2.5/set, cookware pieces USD 2–8/pc, and food storage container sets USD 0.3–1.5/set. Ranges vary with quantity, material and decoration.

Is food-contact compliance needed for stock tableware?

Yes. Stock goods carry the same food-contact rules as first-run goods: EU 1935/2004 with EU 84/500/EC ceramic lead/cadmium limits in the EU, FDA food-contact requirements in the US, and LFGB in Germany. Ask for the original export order's test reports — lots made for EU or US buyers often already have them.

How do I avoid breakage when importing ceramic and glass stock?

Ask for carton weight and packing spec, request a random drop-test video of packed cartons, and check whether inner boxes use dividers rather than loose nesting. Excess air per carton usually means display packing, not re-shipping — repacking cost should be priced into your offer.

What MOQ should I expect for tableware stock?

Because the goods are already fired, glazed and packed, many lots start from one pallet or a few hundred cartons. On stock-clearance.ai many tableware and kitchenware listings show MOQ from 100 pieces or sets, and mixed-pattern lots are common.
