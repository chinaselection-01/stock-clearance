Hotel Supplies & Guest Amenities Stock Lots — the B2B Buying Guide

What China's hospitality surplus actually looks like on the clearance market — where it comes from (hotel refurbishments, pre-opening over-orders, cancelled exports, packaging refreshes), indicative price bands for slippers, amenity kits, towels, bedding and in-room appliances, the towel GSM and fibre checks, expiry and batch lines on consumables, electrical compliance, and a five-step pre-payment checklist.

Key takeaways.

  • Five surplus doors: hotel refurbishment cycles and pre-opening over-orders, cancelled export orders from the linen and amenities supply belts, packaging and branding refreshes that strand old-logo amenity lines, trade-fair and sample surplus, and promotional or corporate-gift tails.
  • Indicative bands: disposable slippers typically USD 0.1–0.5/pair, dental and shaving kits USD 0.05–0.3/set, bath towels USD 0.5–3/pc, bedding sets USD 3–10/set, electric kettles USD 2–6/pc, hair dryers USD 3–10/pc, trays and organizers USD 0.5–2/pc.
  • The signature check is towel quality math: verify GSM against the claimed grade (hotel bath towels typically about 400 GSM budget to 600 GSM+ premium), confirm fibre composition, and inspect washed lots for yellowing, back-wash grey and compressed-storage odour.
  • Consumables have expiry lines: cosmetic-type amenities need batch codes and remaining shelf life — expired or near-expiry kits price as junk; in-room electricals (kettles, hair dryers) need destination voltage/plug matching and CE-style documents for EU retail.
  • Single-style uniformity cuts both ways: matched sets resell fast into hospitality channels, but a 5,000-pair single-style slipper lot has one audience — match the lot to your channel before committing.

1. Where hotel surplus comes from

China's hospitality supply chain runs through distinct belts: terry towels and bedding from Jiangsu and Zhejiang weaving belts, disposable amenities (slippers, dental kits, combs, vanity sets) largely from the Yangzhou and eastern-China amenities belt, and in-room appliances from the Zhejiang and Guangdong electrical belts. Surplus reaches the clearance market through five doors:

  • Refurbishment cycles and pre-opening over-orders. Hotels re-outfit rooms every few years and buy hedge quantities against occupancy forecasts; renovations retire usable linens and appliances in bulk, and new openings that opened under forecast strand unopened reserves — frequently chain-grade quality, unused or lightly used.
  • Cancelled export orders. Finished towel, bedding or amenity-kit runs that lose their buyer mid-schedule price aggressively — factory-new, newest stock on the clearance market and usually the best documentation carry-over.
  • Packaging and branding refreshes. When a hotel group or amenities brand updates packaging or a property rebrands, entire old-logo amenity lines (shampoo bottles, soap bars, gift boxes) strand as new, in-date stock. These lots typically clear deepest because the logo limits the resale audience.
  • Trade-fair and sample surplus. Hotel-supply fair samples and showroom lines retire in single-model lots — new condition, sometimes with display wear.
  • Promotional and corporate-gift tails. Branded slippers, toiletry kits and textile giveaways produced for campaigns and events clear as one-off single-SKU lots, typically 2,000–20,000 pieces.

3. The signature check: towel GSM and fibre math

Terry goods are priced by fabric weight, and stock lots live or die on this math:

  • Verify GSM, not claims. Hotel bath towels typically range from about 400 GSM (budget) to 600 GSM and above (premium). Weigh sample towels and compute GSM against the claimed grade — a "premium" claim that weighs like a budget towel is a pricing problem, not a detail.
  • Confirm fibre content. 100% cotton commands the hospitality premium; polyester blends wash harder and dry faster but resell into lower channels. Check woven-in labels or composition certificates — export lots often carry them.
  • Grade washed lots hard. Refurbishment residue is frequently washed: inspect for yellowing, grey back-wash residue (mineral deposits from industrial laundry), edge fray and compressed-storage odour. Unopened cancelled-export lots avoid all of these — they earn the top of the range.
  • Count the size run. Towel lots strand in odd ratios (excess hand towels, short face towels). Map piece counts per size against your channel before pricing — the size-run trap mirrors the footwear size-curve problem.

4. Consumables, expiry and the cosmetic line

  • Amenity kits are regulated consumables. Shampoo, body lotion, soap and bath-gel contents are cosmetics in most destination markets: labels typically need batch codes and, practically, remaining shelf life that makes the kit sellable. Expired or near-expiry kits should be priced as junk or skipped — no channel rescues them economically.
  • Old-logo lines trade on disclosure. Rebranded-hotel stock is new and in-date but carries the old identity. Buyers should confirm what the packaging says, because scraping or over-labelling branded packaging is not a legitimate path — resale of legitimately marketed goods relies on trademark exhaustion, never re-labelling.
  • Battery-powered amenity devices. LED vanity mirrors, electric aromatherapy units and similar items with lithium cells follow the lithium-battery shipping line (UN38.3 test summary, MSDS, watt-hour ratings) covered in the electronics surplus guide. Lots without documents ship as general cargo only if cells are removed or absent.
  • Textile compliance as reference. Hospitality textiles reference skin-contact safety regimes; lots from export chains often carry OEKO-TEX-style or destination-market test carry-over — worth asking for, and a pricing plus when present.

5. In-room electricals and mixed-lot logistics

  • Voltage and plug matching. Kettles and hair dryers follow the small-appliance electrical line: 110–120 V markets (North America, parts of LATAM) versus 220–240 V markets (EU, UK, GCC, AU), with matching plugs and CE-style documentation for EU retail. The destination voltage table in the small appliance guide applies directly.
  • Function-test per sample. Boil-test kettles through full cycles (auto shut-off is the failure point), run hair dryers through heat/cool cycles and check safety cut-offs. Mechanisms fail in ways photos hide.
  • Uniformity is the feature — and the trap. Matched sets (one slipper style, one bedding colourway) resell fast into hospitality and re-outfitting channels. For retail resale, single-style lots have a single audience: match the lot to your channel before committing — the discipline covered in the reselling channel guide.
  • Freight reality. Towels and bedding cube heavily when loose; ask whether cartons are compressed-packed (common for export terry goods). Kettles and dryers stack in standard cartons; compute freight per unit before negotiating. Mixed amenity-plus-electrical lots consolidate well — see the mixed container guide.
  • Trademark note. Branded amenity lines (hotel-group logos, toiletry brands) carry trademarks owned by their owners — resale is typically covered by trademark exhaustion only when goods entered the market legitimately; never re-label, and audit branded items before committing.

6. How to buy: five-step checklist

Step 1 — Identify the door

Cancelled exports and pre-opening reserves are factory-new and documentation-friendly; refurbishment residue needs wash-and-wear grading; old-logo rebrand lines need identity disclosure.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • carton photos including size labels, GSM tags and composition labels (textiles) or model plates (electricals);
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card, showing a carton opened from the middle of the stack and a sample towel weighed / kettle boiled;
  • batch codes and remaining shelf life for cosmetic contents, and voltage/CE-style documents for electricals.

Step 3 — Weigh, test and smell

Weigh sample towels against GSM claims, boil-test kettles and cycle hair dryers, and physically inspect washed textiles for yellowing, back-wash grey and storage odour — none of which photographs well.

Step 4 — Map the size run and the channel

Count pieces per size and per style against the packing list, then confirm your channel can absorb the specific size run and the single-style uniformity before pricing.

Step 5 — Compute freight per unit first

Get compressed-carton dimensions for textiles and stacked-carton dimensions for appliances, compute CBM per unit and freight per unit for your destination, then negotiate on landed cost.

Where do hotel supplies stock lots come from?

The main doors are hotel refurbishment cycles and pre-opening over-orders (hotels buy hedge quantities against occupancy forecasts), cancelled export orders from the linen, bedding and amenities supply belts, packaging and branding refreshes that strand old-logo amenity lines, trade-fair and sample surplus, and promotional or corporate-gift tails such as branded slippers and toiletry kits.

What do hotel amenity and linen stock lots typically cost?

Indicative clearance ranges: disposable slippers typically USD 0.1–0.5 per pair, dental and shaving kits USD 0.05–0.3 per set, bath towels USD 0.5–3 per piece, bedding sets USD 3–10 per set, electric kettles USD 2–6 per unit, hair dryers USD 3–10 per unit, and trays or amenity organizers USD 0.5–2 per piece. Actual prices depend on quantity, condition and whether consumables are in date.

What should buyers check on hotel towel and linen lots?

Verify fabric weight (GSM) against the claimed grade — hotel bath towels typically range from about 400 GSM (budget) to 600 GSM and above (premium); confirm fibre content (100% cotton versus blends) on labels or composition certificates; inspect washed lots for yellowing, grey back-wash residue and compressed-storage odour; and count pieces per size because towel lots often strand in odd size ratios.

Do consumable amenity kits have expiry or compliance lines?

Yes. Cosmetic-type amenities (shampoo, body lotion, soap) are regulated cosmetics in most destination markets and typically need batch codes and remaining shelf life on the label; expired or near-expiry lots should be priced as junk or skipped. Battery-powered amenity devices follow the lithium-battery shipping line (UN38.3, MSDS), and in-room electricals such as kettles and hair dryers need destination voltage and plug matching with CE-style documentation for EU retail.

Why does single-style uniformity matter in hotel stock lots?

Hotel goods are produced in matched sets — one slipper style, one towel size run, one bedding colourway per order. Uniformity is a feature for hospitality buyers re-outfitting rooms, but a trap for retail resellers: a single-style slipper lot of thousands of pairs has one audience, so buyers should match the lot to their resale channel before committing.
