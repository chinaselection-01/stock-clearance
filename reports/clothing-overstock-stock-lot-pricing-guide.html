Clothing Overstock & Apparel Stock Lots — the B2B Pricing Guide

Where garment surplus comes from, indicative per-piece price bands, how A/B grading works for apparel, the size-run trap, and a pre-payment checklist for importers, discount retailers and online resellers.

Key takeaways.

  • Clothing overstock is structural, not accidental: cancelled export orders, overproduction buffers and size-run breaks keep the surplus channel supplied year-round.
  • Indicative discount: well-bought apparel stock lots typically clear 50–80% below comparable new-production wholesale.
  • Indicative bands: basic tees USD 0.3–1.2/pc, hoodies USD 1.5–4, jeans USD 2–5, jackets USD 2–6 (indicative, varies with grade and season).
  • The size curve is the #1 hidden risk: lots that are all S/M or all XL clear far below normal-priced lots — always ask for the size breakdown before pricing.
  • MOQ is soft by nature: goods are finished, so many apparel lots start from one pallet or from 100 pcs on stock-clearance.ai.

1. Where clothing overstock comes from

China's apparel belts — Guangdong (Guangzhou, Dongguan, Zhongshan), Zhejiang (Ningbo, Huzhou, Yiwu), Jiangsu and Shandong — sew for the whole world. Surplus keeps flowing into the stock-lot channel through five recurring doors:

  • Cancelled or unclaimed export orders. A finished order that misses its shipping window or fails the buyer's payment becomes the factory's inventory.
  • Overproduction buffers. Factories sew 2–5% above order quantity against shrinkage; the extra is sold off quietly after delivery.
  • End-of-season residue. Domestic chains liquidate last season's goods fast to free shelf space, and the goods enter the secondary market in bulk.
  • Size-run breaks. When a retailer sells through the common sizes, the leftover S/XL/XXL tail becomes a surplus lot at a steep discount.
  • Packaging and label changes. A rebrand or new hangtag makes finished garments unsellable in the original channel — the garments themselves are first quality.

For an importer this means apparel is one of the categories where buying below new-production cost is structurally normal, not a one-off windfall.

3. Grading apparel stock: A, B and mixed

Apparel grading is looser than electronics but the vocabulary matters in negotiation:

  • A grade: first quality, current or recent season, complete with labels and packaging — usually overproduction or cancelled exports. Priced highest, sells fastest.
  • B grade: minor defects — light soiling from storage, small stitching flaws, missing hangtags. Typically 20–40% below A grade and suitable for discount retail or export to price-sensitive markets.
  • Mixed / unsorted: sold by carton or per kilogram, contents as-is. Highest margin if you can sort and grade yourself; most dangerous if you cannot.

Rule of thumb: if you cannot inspect or sort the goods yourself, pay for A grade. The price gap between A and mixed lots is smaller than the loss from a container of unsorted stock you cannot move.

4. The two traps specific to clothing lots

Trap 1 — the size curve

A lot priced at "USD 1.50/pc, 20,000 pcs" can be 80% size S or 80% XXL. Both clear far below a balanced curve. Always demand the size breakdown per style before pricing, and match it to your market: EU buyers typically need a mid-heavy curve (M/L), while some export markets skew smaller or larger.

Trap 2 — labelling and compliance

  • Fibre composition and care labels must match your destination market's textile labelling rules (for the EU, regulation-based fibre and origin labelling; for the US, FTC textile rules). Missing or wrong labels usually mean relabelling costs — price it in.
  • Children's garments face extra rules: drawstring and small-parts safety, and in some markets flammability standards for sleepwear. Inspect kids' lots hardest.
  • Branded garments (export overruns carrying a brand's labels): trademarks belong to their owners. Stock lots are surplus, not authorised first-run retail goods — confirm your market's trademark resale rules before importing.

5. How to buy: a five-step pre-payment checklist

Step 1 — Fix the size curve first

Ask for per-style size breakdowns and carton packing lists before discussing price. This one document eliminates the most common bad buy.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card;
  • carton photos including inner packing and a random open box;
  • storage conditions — apparel stored in damp warehouses develops mould that photos may hide.

Step 3 — Sample-test before container volume

Buy one carton or one pallet first. Check GSM weight, seams, zippers and how the garments smell out of the box. Then scale up.

Step 4 — Inspect anything above a pallet

Third-party inspection in Guangzhou, Ningbo or Yiwu costs typically USD 100–200 per man-day. The most common findings on apparel lots are wrong size ratios and mixed-grade cartons — exactly the things an inspector catches.

Step 5 — Negotiate the lot, not the piece

Stock sellers clear warehouses. "I take the whole lot, ex-warehouse, this week" outperforms per-piece haggling, and mixing apparel with shoes, bags or household goods from 2–3 suppliers into one container typically improves landed cost by double digits.

Frequently asked questions

Where does clothing overstock in China come from?

The main sources are cancelled or unclaimed export orders, factory overproduction buffers, end-of-season retail residue, size-run breaks, and packaging or label changes that make finished garments unsellable in the original channel.

What price can buyers expect for apparel stock lots?

Well-bought clothing stock lots typically clear at 50–80% below comparable new-production wholesale. Indicative ranges: basic tees around USD 0.3–1.2/pc, hoodies USD 1.5–4, jeans USD 2–5, dresses USD 1–4, jackets USD 2–6 — varying with grade, season and quantity.

What is the MOQ for clothing stock lots?

Garments are already sewn, so MOQ logic is soft: many lots start from one pallet or a few hundred pieces. On stock-clearance.ai many apparel listings show MOQ from 100 pieces; mixed lots are often quoted per kilogram or per carton.

What should I check before paying?

Check the size curve, demand carton-level packing lists, look for moisture or mould in storage photos, verify care labels and fibre composition for your destination market, and book third-party inspection (typically USD 100–200 per man-day) for larger orders.

Can I buy branded clothing surplus for resale?

Branded surplus exists — for example OEM export overruns carrying a brand's labels — but trademarks belong to their owners and resale rules differ by market. Stock lots are surplus goods, not authorised first-run retail goods, so confirm your market's trademark rules before importing branded apparel.
