Mixed Clothing & Footwear Stock Lots — the B2B Pallet Buying Guide

What mixed apparel-and-shoe stock actually looks like on the clearance market — why garments and shoes get combined into single lots, indicative price bands and per-piece pricing logic, the speed-grading checks (size runs, pair integrity, season mixing), freight and channel fit, and a five-step pre-payment checklist.

Key takeaways.

  • Mixed lots are a packaging format, not a category: garments and shoes get combined because they came from the same cancelled fashion order, the same store liquidation, or the same sorting house — the format rewards buyers with sorting capacity.
  • Indicative effective bands: mixed apparel-dominant pallets typically USD 0.5–2.5 per unit, shoe-dominant mixes USD 1.5–4 per unit, balanced mixes USD 0.8–3 per unit — read every pallet price as a per-unit price before comparing.
  • The mix ratio is the deal: a 70/30 garment-to-shoe pallet and a 30/70 pallet of the same nominal grade are different businesses — always demand a category-mix breakdown before quoting.
  • Footwear drives freight: shoes weigh several times what garments do per unit — shoe-dominant pallets need per-kg freight math, not per-cbm math, and the effective landed cost shifts accordingly.
  • Season mixing is the hidden variable: summer tees and winter coats in one lot clear through different channels in different months — confirm the season split, because it sets your resale calendar, not just your price.

1. Why clothing and footwear end up in the same lot

Garments and shoes are different factories, different HS lines and different QC regimes — so when they appear in one lot, there is always a structural reason. The four common doors:

  • Cancelled fashion-range exports. A fashion buyer's order typically covers clothes and matching footwear together; when the order is cancelled or misses the sailing window, the finished container strands as a natural mixed lot with a coherent size story.
  • Retail-store liquidations. When a store closes or resets, everything on the floor clears at once — apparel racks and shoe walls get packed into the same pallets, usually in original retail packaging.
  • Sorting-house re-packing. Sorting operations buy single-category leftovers, grade them, and re-pack the tails into combined pallets. These lots are pre-sorted by condition but can be wildly mixed by season and style.
  • Distributor overstock bundling. Wholesalers bundle slow-moving apparel tails with footwear tails to move both at one price — the bundling itself signals that neither category moved well on its own.

Practical implication: always ask which door produced the lot. Cancelled exports and store liquidations typically carry the cleanest documentation and packaging; sorting-house mixes need the deepest inspection.

3. Pricing logic: read pallet prices as per-unit prices

Mixed lots get quoted per pallet, per kg or per piece — and the three are not comparable until you convert. The working method:

  • Convert everything to per-unit. Divide the pallet price by the total unit count (garment units + shoe pairs). A USD 600 pallet with 400 units is USD 1.5/unit; the same pallet with 150 units is USD 4/unit — same photos, different business.
  • Weight the mix. Shoes typically weigh 2–4x what a garment unit weighs. In shoe-dominant mixes, freight per unit can rival the goods cost — compute freight on the actual packing list, not on a per-cbm rule of thumb.
  • Price the unknown. Unsorted cancelled-export cartons are priced deepest for a reason: the size run and style mix inside are unknown until opened. Experienced buyers either demand a sample carton opening on video or apply a discount that reflects the uncertainty.
  • Compare against single-category benchmarks. Cross-check the effective per-unit price against the single-category bands for clothing overstock (indicative USD 0.3–6/pc by garment type) and footwear stock lots (indicative USD 0.5–12/pair by style). If the mixed-lot price is not clearly below the blended single-category price, the bundle carries no discount for the added complexity.

4. Speed-grading a mixed pallet

You cannot grade 5,000 mixed units individually — buyers grade by sampling. The five checks that matter, in the order that kills deals fastest:

  • Pair integrity on footwear. Shoes strand as singles when cartons are opened and repacked. Sample-check cartons for matched pairs (left+right, same size) and both boxes. Single shoes are near-worthless in most channels.
  • Size distribution across both categories. Mid-sizes (the sellable core) disappear first in any sorted lot. Ask for the size breakdown of garments and shoes separately; a lot skewed to XS and XXXL is a discount-channel lot at a discount-channel price.
  • Season mixing. Summer tees and winter coats in one lot need different resale months. Get the season split in writing — it defines your selling calendar and cash-flow cycle, and heavily mixed seasons justify a further discount.
  • Condition sampling by carton, not by pallet. Pull samples from the top, middle and bottom of the stack. Bottom-carton goods in floor-stacked pallets carry compression damage, damp odour and staining that top-carton samples hide.
  • Label and brand audit. List which brands and private labels appear. Unbranded generic goods clear freely; branded goods follow the trademark-exhaustion line — original labels stay, no re-labelling, and well-known brands without documentation get skipped.

6. How to buy: five-step checklist

Step 1 — Identify the door and the mix ratio

Ask which door produced the lot (cancelled export, store liquidation, sorting-house re-pack, distributor bundle) and demand a written category breakdown: garment units vs shoe pairs, by season. No breakdown, no offer.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • full pallet photos including bottom layers and packaging types;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card, opening at least one carton;
  • a size and brand list for both the apparel and the footwear portion.

Step 3 — Sample from the bottom

Pull samples from top, middle and bottom cartons. Check pair integrity, stains, odour and sole wear on footwear, and seam and label condition on garments. What the top of the pallet shows is marketing; what the bottom shows is the deal.

Step 4 — Run the landed-cost math

Convert the quote to per-unit, run freight on the actual gross weight (shoe-dominant pallets are heavy), and compare the landed per-unit cost against single-category benchmarks. Skip bundles that price above their blended parts.

Step 5 — Match the lot to your channel before paying

Confirm your resale channel absorbs the season split and the size skew. If your channel is curated, buy Grade-A sorted mixes at a premium; if your channel sorts in-house, unsorted lots pay you for the work.

Frequently asked questions

What is a mixed clothing and footwear stock lot?

A single lot combining garment units (tops, bottoms, outerwear) with pairs of shoes or boots, usually packed on shared pallets. The mix typically comes from cancelled export orders that covered a full fashion range, retail-store liquidations that clear all categories at once, or sorting houses that re-pack leftover apparel and footwear tails into combined pallets.

How are mixed clothing and footwear lots priced?

Typically per piece or per pallet. Indicative effective ranges are roughly USD 0.5–2.5 per unit for apparel-dominant mixes, USD 1.5–4 per unit for shoe-dominant mixes and USD 0.8–3 per unit for balanced mixes. Always convert pallet quotes to per-unit prices — the same pallet can be USD 1.5 or USD 4 per unit depending on the count.

What should I check before buying a mixed pallet?

Ask for a category-mix breakdown (garment units vs shoe pairs), check size distribution across both categories, verify shoes are complete matched pairs with both boxes, confirm the season split (summer and winter goods sell in different months), and sample from bottom cartons for stains, odour and sole wear.

Are branded items in mixed lots safe to buy?

Branded and private-label items are legitimate liquidation goods, but trademarks belong to their owners — treat under trademark-exhaustion practice, keep original labelling and never re-label or re-box. Skip lots carrying well-known consumer brands without documentation, or price in the risk.

Who buys mixed clothing and footwear stock lots?

Discount retailers, market stalls, live-commerce sellers and online resellers whose model includes sorting mixed goods. Specialised single-category retailers usually prefer single-category lots with a clean size run — the mixed format trades price for sorting work.
