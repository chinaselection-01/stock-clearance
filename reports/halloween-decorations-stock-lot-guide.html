Halloween Decorations & Party Supplies Stock Lots — the B2B Buying Guide

What China's Halloween surplus actually looks like on the clearance market — where it comes from (post-Halloween retailer residue, party-store tails, cancelled exports), the November buying window and its hold-to-next-season trade-off, indicative price bands by product type, the character-costume IP trap, flammability and LED compliance lines, and a five-step pre-payment checklist.

Key takeaways.

  • November is the buying window: right after Halloween, US and EU retailer residue liquidates at the deepest discounts of the year — post-season buying means holding roughly ten months, so the discount must outweigh storage and capital cost.
  • Indicative bands: plastic décor and paper goods typically USD 0.05–0.5/pc, light-up props USD 0.5–3/pc, costumes USD 1–5/pc, inflatables and large props USD 3–12/pc, party tableware USD 0.1–0.6/pc or pack.
  • Character prints are the IP trap: costumes and décor printed with film, TV and game characters are the classic unauthorized-merchandise zone — skip character lots entirely; generic themes (witches, skeletons, ghosts, pumpkins) dominate legitimate supply.
  • Two compliance lines: costume flammability for destination markets (US 16 CFR 1610 for wearing apparel; EN 71-2 commonly referenced for kids costume-toys in the EU) and battery documentation for LED light-up props.
  • Age shows in the material: paper and latex yellow and embrittle, foam props pick up damp-storage odour, LEDs fail after long storage — bend-test, smell-test and light-test samples from different cartons before paying.

1. Why Halloween surplus exists at all

China's holiday-goods belts — Yiwu and Cangnan in Zhejiang, plus Shantou and Dongguan in Guangdong — produce most of the world's Halloween décor, costumes and party supplies. Surplus enters through five doors:

  • Post-Halloween retailer residue. The biggest door. US and EU chains buy hard for a late-October demand window that ends overnight on 1 November; unsold stock clears in November regardless of margin, often as full-assortment mixed lots.
  • Party-store and wholesaler tails. Costume shops and party wholesalers carry deep assortments for one night a year; their end-of-season leftovers liquidate as mixed cartons.
  • Cancelled export orders. Finished containers that miss the sailing window. Halloween goods are unseasonal the moment the window passes, so stranded lots price aggressively — but they are universal-fit and sellable in any Halloween market.
  • Event and promotional tails. Bars, malls, theme parks and FMCG campaigns order themed props and giveaways; unused event stock liquidates after the event, often single-SKU.
  • Packaging refreshes. A new retail-box design strands finished goods in old packaging — product unchanged, typically priced at a packaging discount.

3. The November window and the hold-to-next-season trade-off

Halloween demand is one night a year, which makes the buying clock unusually sharp:

TimingWhat's availableTrade-off
November (post-season)deepest supply and lowest prices of the year; full retailer residuehold roughly ten months before the next demand window — discount must outweigh storage and capital cost
Spring–summer (counter-season)factory overproduction, cancelled exports, packaging-refresh lotsbetter cash-flow timing, thinner selection, moderately higher prices
August–October (in-season)whatever residue remainsprices firm up fast; late buyers pay for urgency

Halloween styling changes slowly year to year — ghosts, skeletons and pumpkins do not go out of fashion — so aged stock holds resale value better than trend-driven décor. The two things that do not age well are licensed character prints (an IP problem, not a fashion problem) and the materials themselves, which is why grading checks matter more for Halloween than for most seasonal categories.

4. The character-print IP trap

Halloween is the seasonal category with the highest unauthorized-merchandise risk, because costume and décor designs copy film, TV and game characters:

  • Character-print lots: skip without documents. Genuine licensed costume and décor surplus almost never reaches the open clearance market in bulk. Lots advertised with recognizable franchise characters are frequently unauthorized — importing them exposes the buyer to seizure and trademark liability at destination.
  • Generic themes are the safe core. Witches, skeletons, ghosts, pumpkins, spiders and blood-print designs carry no IP exposure and dominate legitimate supply — the bulk of the November liquidation wave.
  • Own-brand promotional tails. Event and campaign props carry the campaign owner's logo or mascot; treat under trademark-exhaustion practice, confirm chain of title and never re-label or re-box.

5. Grading Halloween stock: materials and lights

Grading follows the A/B/mixed convention used across the stock-lot market, with five category-specific checks:

  • Paper and latex aging. Aged paper goods yellow and embrittle; latex masks and balloons degrade, stick and crack — open cartons from different pallet layers and flex-test samples.
  • Foam and fabric odour. Foam tombstones and costumes stored damp carry a permanent musty smell that photographs well but sells badly — smell-test samples across the lot and downgrade damp stock.
  • LED function tests. Light-up props fail after long storage (battery leakage, corroded contacts, dead circuits). Light-test every variant type, check for leaking batteries inside the props and collect battery documentation.
  • Inflatable blower and seams. Inflate-test yard inflatables with the blower running; check seam splits, fan noise and zip ties. Replacement blowers are rarely available on stock lots.
  • Costume size-run mix. Mid-sizes (M/L) sell first; lots heavy on XS or XXL clear slower. Count the size mix per carton and price your exit accordingly, and confirm flammability documents for destination markets.

6. How to buy: five-step checklist

Step 1 — Identify the door

Ask which door produced the lot. Retailer residue and cancelled exports are the cleanest and most deeply priced; event tails need logo checks; any lot with recognizable character prints needs to be skipped.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • full carton photos including retail-box variants and the size/theme mix;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card;
  • storage history and production dates — paper, latex and foam goods are the most age-sensitive lines in seasonal stock.

Step 3 — Hand-test materials and lights

Flex-test paper and latex, smell-test foam and fabric, light-test every LED variant and inflate-test blowers. Material aging and dead lights are invisible in photos; a ten-minute sample test protects the whole lot.

Step 4 — Verify IP and compliance lines

Skip character-print lots without licence documents; collect costume flammability papers (US 16 CFR 1610 for wearing apparel, EN 71-2 commonly referenced for kids costume-toys in the EU) and battery documentation for LED props.

Step 5 — Plan the exit before you buy

November-bought stock holds roughly ten months: price the discount against storage plus capital cost, plan sell-through into the next Halloween window (resale channels typically start stocking July–August), and consolidate light, cube-efficient Halloween cartons with other seasonal and household stock in one mixed load.

Frequently asked questions

Where do Halloween decoration stock lots come from?

The main doors are post-Halloween retailer residue (US and EU chains clearing unsold seasonal stock in November), party-store tails, cancelled export orders from the Zhejiang and Guangdong holiday-goods belts, event and promotional tails, and packaging refreshes.

What do Halloween stock lots typically cost?

Indicative clearance ranges are roughly USD 0.05–0.5/pc for plastic décor and paper goods, USD 0.5–3/pc for light-up props, USD 1–5/pc for costumes, USD 3–12/pc for inflatables and large props, and USD 0.1–0.6/pc or pack for party tableware. Actual prices depend on quantity, condition and market.

When is the best time to buy?

Typically November, right after Halloween, when retailer residue liquidates at the deepest discounts of the year. The trade-off is holding roughly ten months to the next demand window, so the discount must outweigh storage and capital cost — Halloween styling changes slowly, so aged generic stock holds resale value well.

Are character costumes and licensed decorations safe to buy?

No, not without documents. Film, TV and game character prints are the classic unauthorized-merchandise trap — genuine licensed surplus almost never reaches the open clearance market in bulk. Skip character lots; generic themes (witches, skeletons, ghosts, pumpkins) carry no IP exposure and dominate legitimate supply.

What compliance and grading checks matter most?

Compliance: costume flammability for destination markets (US 16 CFR 1610; EN 71-2 commonly referenced for kids costume-toys in the EU) and battery documentation for LED props. Grading: paper and latex aging, foam odour, light-function tests on every LED variant, inflatable blower tests and costume size-run mix.
