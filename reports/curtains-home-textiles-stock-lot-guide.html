Curtains & Home Textiles Stock Lots — the B2B Buying Guide

Blackout curtains, sheer panels, shower curtains, cushions, bed and table linen and fabric rolls on the surplus market — indicative price ranges, the made-to-measure size trap, mildew and dye-lot grading checks, flame-retardancy documentation lines and a five-step buying checklist for importers, textile wholesalers and discount retailers.

Key takeaways.

  • Textile surplus runs on colour and refurbishment clocks: chain colourway discontinuations, hotel drape rotations, cancelled made-to-measure orders and mill overproduction — not seasonality.
  • Indicative clearance ranges: sheer panels typically USD 0.5–2/pc, ready-made blackout panels USD 1–4/pc, shower curtains USD 0.5–2/pc, cushions USD 0.5–2/pc, bed linen sets USD 2–8/set, fabric rolls USD 0.5–3/metre.
  • The made-to-measure trap: custom-size panels have almost no resale demand — value only the standard-size content and price the odd sizes at zero.
  • Mildew and damp odour are the #1 value killers in stored textiles: smell and inspect fold lines before checking anything else.
  • Contract channels pay a premium for flame-retardancy documents: B1-class or BS 5867-style certification unlocks hotel and institutional resale; without it, lots are restricted to domestic channels.

1. Where curtain and home textile surplus comes from

Window treatments and decorative textiles are colour-, size- and refurbishment-driven. Five doors recur:

  • Chain colourway discontinuations. Interior and curtain chains refresh colour ranges seasonally; outgoing colours clear at deep cuts, usually as matched pairs in standard sizes.
  • Hotel and apartment refurbishment cycles. Hotels and serviced apartments rotate drapes and linen on multi-year cycles — uniform, contract-grade goods, sometimes with flame-retardancy papers.
  • Cancelled made-to-measure orders. Custom panels produced for a specific window and never claimed — the largest and most deceptive surplus door in this category.
  • Cancelled export orders. Finished containers that miss the sailing window or lose their buyer become surplus, often with original packaging intact.
  • Fabric mill and converter overproduction. Running-meter rolls from loom overruns and pattern discontinuations — the raw-material door for converters and workshop channels.

3. The made-to-measure trap: the biggest pricing error in this category

Made-to-measure panels look like the deepest discount in the textile aisle — and often are the worst deal. Custom panels were cut for a specific window: odd heights, single panels instead of pairs, and non-standard drops have almost no resale demand, because most buyers need matched pairs in the standard size ladder.

  • Value only the standard-size content: sort the lot into standard-size pairs (resellable) and odd/custom sizes (residual). Price the residual near zero.
  • A lot that is 70% custom is worth 30% of its apparent volume: deep-looking discounts on custom-heavy lots routinely net out worse than full-price standard lots.
  • Odd sizes do have niche exits: workshop channels, caravan and boat fittings, tier and café rails, remnant retail — but at fractions of standard-size pricing, and slowly.
  • Ask for a size breakdown before quoting: a per-panel size table (height × width, piece counts) is a reasonable ask and the only way to price a mixed lot honestly.

4. Grading checks specific to curtains and textiles

  • Smell first. Mildew and damp-storage odour are the number-one value killers in stored textiles; the smell lives in fold lines and does not wash out economically. Open cartons and sniff before anything else.
  • Check fold lines and creasing: long-folded panels develop permanent fold-line shine and deep creases that steaming only partly removes.
  • Colour fade: showroom-displayed panels and roll ends fade in sunlight; compare exposed edges against inner-carton stock.
  • Dye-lot consistency: the same SKU from different dye batches shows visible colour mismatch when hung side by side — verify the lot comes from one batch, or price it as mixed.
  • Hardware completeness: grommets, rings, hooks and tiebacks go missing between cancellation and resale; count them.
  • Per-panel size verification: labels lie on cancelled orders; measure a sample of panels against the declared sizes.
  • Blackout-coating age: old coatings crack, flake and peel — flex a fold and inspect the coating surface under light.
  • Fibre-composition labels: destination markets enforce labelling; verify content labels exist and are legible on resale packaging.

5. The compliance line: documents that decide the channel

  • Contract channels pay a premium for flame-retardancy documents: hotels, care homes, schools and offices typically require drapery fabrics to hold flame-retardancy certification (B1-class or BS 5867-style documents are common benchmarks). Contract-grade lots with papers command higher prices; stock without documents is restricted to domestic resale channels.
  • Material-claim verification: OEKO-TEX-style and fibre-content claims must exist as verifiable documents or legible labels, not as listing text alone.
  • Destination-market labelling: fibre-composition and care labels are legally required on textile resale in the EU, US and UK; lots missing labels face relabelling costs — price that in.
  • Shower curtains: some markets restrict certain plasticised PVC/PEVA formulations; ask for material declarations on plastic-curtain lots.

Trademark note: some surplus lots carry retail-chain or brand private-label printing on packaging or woven labels. Such marks belong to their owners; lots are secondary-market stock, not authorised first-run goods. Re-labelling branded goods is not acceptable practice; confirm your market's resale rules before importing.

6. How to buy: a five-step checklist

Step 1 — Get the size and batch breakdown before the price

A per-panel size table and dye-batch declaration is the only honest basis for pricing a mixed curtain lot. No table, no quote.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • date of manufacture or batch;
  • full carton photos incl. fold condition and hardware;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card.

Step 3 — Smell and touch before you negotiate

Mildew odour and cracked blackout coating cannot be fixed at scale. One sniff of an opened carton tells you more than ten photos.

Step 4 — Sort custom from standard, then price the standard

Value only matched pairs in standard sizes. Custom and odd-size content is residual — price it near zero or plan a remnant channel.

Step 5 — Inspect anything above a pallet

Third-party inspection typically costs USD 100–200 per man-day: dye-lot mixing and hidden mildew cartons are the two most common findings on textile lots.
