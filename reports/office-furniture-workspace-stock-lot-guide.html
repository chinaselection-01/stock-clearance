1. Where office furniture surplus comes from

China's office-furniture belt — Anji in Zhejiang for chairs (the world's largest chair-production county), Foshan and Luoyang for desks, cabinets and fit-out lines — supplies offices, hotels, schools and export chains. Surplus enters through five doors:

  • Office closures and tenant exits. Companies downsizing or exiting premises liquidate whole-floor fit-outs in bulk: rows of identical task chairs, desks and pedestals in working condition, often at a fraction of contract price. Uniformity is the feature — matched sets resell faster than mixed lots.
  • Showroom and trade-fair discontinued models. Chair and desk makers refresh showroom lines annually; retired display and discontinued models clear as new-condition single-model lots, sometimes with minor display wear.
  • Hotel and office refurbishment cycles. Renovation schedules retire usable furniture in bulk every few years — frequently chain-grade quality with intact fabrics.
  • Cancelled export orders. Finished chair or workstation runs that lose their buyer mid-schedule price aggressively — often the newest stock on the clearance market and, unlike used fit-outs, genuinely unopened.
  • Project overruns. Fit-out tenders over-produced against floor plans (hedge quantities, changed layouts); the residue is new, single-model and matched — ideal for resale into project and secondary channels.

3. The signature check: the gas lift

The gas lift is both the most common failure point and the biggest hidden value variable in chair lots:

  • Sit-test through full stroke. Raise and lower sample chairs through the full stroke several times under load. Failing lifts sink slowly under seated weight, stick mid-stroke, or hiss — none of which photographs.
  • Verify certification class. Quality task chairs typically use Class 3 or Class 4 certified lifts; unbranded uncertified lifts are the usual shortcut in cheap lots. Ask which class the lot uses — a certified-lift claim should be checkable on the cylinder marking.
  • Price the lift, not the chair. Lift replacement costs a meaningful share of a clearance chair's price, so a lot with a known lift problem should be priced accordingly — or walked away from. Used fit-out lots deserve more lift testing than cancelled-export lots (which are factory-new).
  • Parallel chair checks: mesh sagging (stretched mesh loses lumbar support permanently), armrest cracking (aged plastic, especially PU), castor condition, and base-star cracking at the hub.

4. The flat-pack hardware trap

Most export office furniture ships flat-packed, which creates a trap specific to stock lots:

  • Kits, not products. Desks, cabinets and shelving are sold as kits: cam locks, dowels, screws, handles, castors and tools ship inside the cartons. A lot missing even one hardware bag type effectively strands the goods — sourcing matching hardware separately rarely makes economic sense.
  • Count per carton sample. Open cartons from the middle of the stack and count hardware bags against the packing list. Check that panels are unbattered (edge chips, screw-hole tears from disassembly are common in used fit-out lots).
  • Assembly-document reality. Resale channels for flat-pack stock include the buyer's own customers needing assembly instructions; lots without instruction sheets or QR-linked manuals sell into professional-assembly channels at a discount. The same set-completeness discipline covered in the home storage guide applies to modular furniture.

5. Compliance and freight lines

  • Wood-panel formaldehyde. Composite panels (particleboard, MDF) carry formaldehyde emission regimes: US retail channels typically expect CARB Phase 2 / TSCA Title VI documentation, EU retail expects E1-grade (or better) panels. Undocumented lots can still trade into discount, secondary-market and project channels — but the documentation gap prices as a discount, and many retail chains won't list undocumented panels. Ask for panel certificates before committing to retail-channel volumes.
  • Electrical desks. Height-adjustable desks follow the small-appliance electrical line: destination voltage (110 V vs 220–240 V) and plug matching, controller and motor function testing, and CE-style documentation for EU retail. The voltage trap covered in the small appliance guide applies directly here.
  • Structural standards as reference. Contract and retail channels reference chair strength and durability standards (EN 1335 in Europe, BIFMA X5.1 in North America); stock lots rarely carry full test reports, but lots that came from export chains often inherit partial documentation — worth asking for.
  • Cube-cost freight math. Office furniture is cube-heavy: chairs disassembled into flat-pack cartons typically cube at roughly 0.15–0.25 CBM per carton, and a 40ft HC container fills by volume long before weight. Compute freight per unit before negotiating — a chair price that looks cheap can lose its margin to cube. Desks and tables stack worse than chairs; ask for stacked-carton dimensions.
  • Trademark note. Branded fit-out residue (chains retiring branded furniture) carries trademarks owned by their owners — resale is typically covered by trademark exhaustion only when goods entered the market legitimately; never re-label, and audit branded items before committing.

6. How to buy: five-step checklist

Step 1 — Identify the door

Cancelled exports and project overruns are factory-new and documentation-friendly; used fit-outs need condition grading and more testing; showroom models need display-wear checks. Match your channel to the door.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • carton photos including hardware bags and panel edges (flat-pack) or full-chair photos including cylinder marking (assembled);
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card, showing a carton opened from the middle of the stack and a chair assembled and sit-tested;
  • panel formaldehyde documents for retail-channel volumes, and electrical certificates for standing-desk lines.

Step 3 — Sit-test and function-test

Full stroke-cycle the gas lifts on sample chairs, cycle drawer runners and locks, and run standing desks through full up/down cycles. Mechanisms fail in ways photos hide.

Step 4 — Verify hardware completeness

Count hardware bags, handles, castors and tools per carton sample against the packing list. Confirm instruction sheets or manual links exist if your channel needs them.

Step 5 — Compute freight per unit first

Get stacked-carton dimensions, compute CBM per unit and freight per unit for your destination, then negotiate on landed cost. Furniture margins live and die on cube math — never negotiate ex-works only.

Do wood-panel office furniture lots need formaldehyde documents?

Undocumented lots can still trade into discount, secondary-market and project channels, but the documentation gap prices as a discount and many retail chains will not list undocumented panels at all.

How does gas-lift quality affect chair value?

Chairs with failing or uncertified lifts sell only at deep discount, because lift replacement costs a large share of a clearance chair's price.
