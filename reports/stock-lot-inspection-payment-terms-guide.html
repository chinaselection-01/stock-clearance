Stock Lot Inspection & Payment Terms — the B2B Risk Control Guide

How experienced importers pay for and verify surplus stock from China: typical T/T structures, the three proofs before any deposit, third-party inspection scope and costs, AQL adapted for mixed-grade lots, and the red flags that end negotiations.

Key takeaways.

  • T/T dominates stock-lot payments: a deposit (commonly around 30%) with the balance before shipment or against a B/L copy is the typical structure; letters of credit are rare on one-off stock deals.
  • Pay only to a company account matching the supplier's registered name — never to a personal account, however small the lot.
  • The three proofs before any deposit: full carton photos, a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card, and the date of manufacture.
  • Third-party inspection typically costs USD 100–200 per man-day in the supplier's city; wrong counts and mixed grades are the two most common findings, and both are far cheaper to catch before payment.
  • Standard AQL needs adapting for stock: sample per carton batch, inspect each grade separately, and add set-completeness, size-run and moisture checks that production-run AQL ignores.

1. Why stock-lot risk is different from first-run sourcing

First-run orders fail slowly: a factory misses a production date, and you have weeks to react. Stock lots fail fast and irreversibly — once the goods ship, there is no re-order and no factory to complain to, because the lot was unique. Three structural features drive the risk:

  • No recourse production. A surplus lot is a one-time pool of finished goods. If the count or grade is wrong, there is nothing to "fix in the next batch".
  • Mixed provenance. One warehouse can hold goods from several origins, production dates and quality tiers — sometimes inside one carton range.
  • Speed pressure. Good lots sell in days. The seller's urgency ("three buyers are asking") is real but is also the most-abused sales lever in the trade.

The discipline that resolves this tension: compress verification into days, never skip it. The checklist in section 5 is designed for exactly that.

Patterns are indicative market practice, not offers. Terms are negotiated lot by lot between buyer and supplier on stock-clearance.ai.

Two rules that cut across every structure:

  • Beneficiary name discipline. The bank account must match the supplier's registered company name. A mismatch between the listing, the contract and the beneficiary is the classic payment-fraud signature.
  • Milestone linkage. Tie each payment to a deliverable: deposit ⇢ three proofs + contract; balance ⇢ inspection report or B/L copy. A payment with no deliverable attached is a gift.

3. Pre-payment verification: the three proofs and beyond

The three proofs (minimum standard)

  • Full carton photos — including inner packing, assortment mix and any grade labels.
  • Live warehouse video with a handwritten date card — a scheduled video call where the seller walks the racks; recordings alone can be recycled between deals.
  • Date of manufacture — the age of the lot drives both price and resale claims (batteries, adhesives, food-contact materials, licence status).

Beyond the minimum

  • Physical samples from the actual lot (not the showroom) — courier a few cartons' worth before committing a container.
  • Carton-dimension and pcs/carton data for freight math — bulky goods can destroy margins that looked fine per piece.
  • For branded or licensed goods: confirm in writing that lots are surplus stock, not authorised first-run retail goods; trademarks belong to their owners, and resale rules vary by market.

4. Third-party inspection adapted to stock lots

Third-party inspection in the supplier's city typically costs USD 100–200 per man-day, and on container-size purchases that is usually a fraction of one percent of goods value. Standard production AQL sampling is the starting point, but stock lots break its assumptions — a stock warehouse is not a uniform production run. The practical adaptation:

CheckProduction-run AQLStock-lot adaptation
Sampling frameRandom sample across one production batchSample proportionally from each carton batch / pallet; lots mix batches
Grade controlOne grade definitionInspect each declared grade (A / B / mixed) separately; verify the mix ratio matches the invoice
Count verificationRarely neededCore check — actual pcs per carton vs packing list, on a fixed number of cartons
Set completenessN/AVerify multi-part goods (sets, pairs, kits, hardware bags) are complete
Size-run balanceN/AApparel/footwear: verify the size curve matches what was sold, not just total pieces
Condition & storageN/AMoisture damage, odour, dust, carton crush, date-of-manufacture consistency

The two findings that recur across almost every stock-lot inspection: wrong counts and mixed grades. Both are cheap to catch at inspection and expensive to discover at your warehouse.

5. Red flags that end negotiations

  • Full payment demanded up front on a large lot, with inspection refused or "not necessary".
  • No live warehouse access — only pre-recorded videos or photos that look generic.
  • Price far below every comparable lot without a credible story (the credible stories exist — cancelled exports, rebranding, season ends — but they come with documentation).
  • Beneficiary name mismatch between the listing, the proforma invoice and the bank account.
  • No date of manufacture, or dates that shift between documents.

Any single red flag justifies slowing the deal down; several together justify walking away. The stock-lot market is deep — no single lot is worth a broken rule.

6. The five-step pre-payment checklist

Step 1 — Verify the seller's identity

Company name on listing = company name on contract = bank beneficiary. A registered Chinese company can be checked against its business licence in minutes.

Step 2 — Collect the three proofs

Carton photos, live warehouse video with date card, date of manufacture — before any money moves.

Step 3 — Fix the payment milestones

Deposit tied to proofs and contract; balance tied to inspection report or B/L copy. Company account only.

Step 4 — Inspect proportionally

For container lots, book third-party inspection (typically USD 100–200 per man-day) with the stock-lot scope: counts per carton batch, grade mix, set completeness, size curve, moisture and dates.

Step 5 — Paper the deal

A short written confirmation covering: lot contents and grades, pcs/carton and carton counts, total price and terms, inspection rights, and the surplus/not-first-run status for branded goods. In stock trade the paper trail is the warranty.

Frequently asked questions

What payment terms are typical for China stock lots?

T/T dominates: a deposit (commonly around 30%) with the balance before shipment or against a B/L copy. Full prepayment appears on very small lots; letters of credit are rare because one-off stock deals rarely justify LC fees and document rigidity. Always pay to a company account matching the supplier's registered name.

How much does third-party inspection cost in China?

Typically USD 100–200 per man-day in the supplier's city. A one-day inspection usually covers counts, carton condition, assortment mix, grade sampling and photo documentation — a fraction of one percent of a container's value.

Does standard AQL sampling work for stock lots?

It helps but must be adapted: sample proportionally per carton batch, inspect each grade separately, and add set-completeness, size-run, date-consistency and moisture checks that production-run AQL ignores.

What proof should I get before paying?

The three proofs: full carton photos including inner packing, a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card, and the date of manufacture. For branded goods, add written confirmation that lots are surplus stock, not authorised first-run retail goods.

What are the red flags when buying surplus stock?

Pressure for full payment up front, refusal of live warehouse access, prices far below comparables without a documented story, beneficiary names that don't match the supplier, and missing or shifting manufacture dates.
