  • Indicative pricing typically runs USD 0.2–0.8/pc bandanas, USD 0.5–1.5/pc t-shirts and dresses, USD 1–3/pc sweaters, hoodies and raincoats, USD 2–6/pc insulated winter jackets, USD 0.5–3/pc holiday costumes.

1. Why pet clothing strands as surplus

Pet clothing rides the same Zhejiang textile and pet-goods belts as the rest of the pet channel, but its demand is sharply seasonal and fashion-driven. Five recurring doors create stock lots:

  • The season clock. Pet chains stock winter coats and sweaters for the cold months and clear them hard in February–April; holiday costumes (Christmas reindeer sets, Halloween pumpkins, Santa suits) clear in November–December. Post-season residue is the single biggest source of pet-apparel lots.
  • Subscription-box rotation. Monthly pet boxes commission fresh designs constantly; last month's design becomes surplus the day the box ships.
  • Colourway and assortment resets. Pet chains reset apparel colourways on fashion logic; previous colourways clear at a fraction of first cost.
  • Cancelled export orders. Finished containers of coats and costumes that miss a sailing window or fail buyer funding liquidate at any price.
  • Promotional and packaging tails. Promotional gift runs, trade-fair samples and private-label apparel re-boxed for a new retail brand become surplus the day the branding changes.

This guide covers the apparel line. It complements the existing reports on pet toys, pet feeding products and the general pet supplies overview — together they map the whole pet channel.

3. The sizing chaos trap

Pet clothing has no unified sizing standard, and that single fact shapes the whole category's resale value:

  • Four incompatible sizing systems coexist: back length, chest girth, neck circumference and body weight. The same letter size can differ by several centimetres across makers, and buyers' dogs do not read labels — returns from guessing sizes are the reseller's biggest cost.
  • Demand a measured size chart. Lots sold with a back-length and chest-girth chart per size sell; lots sold as "S–XL assorted" without measurements typically price 20–40% below documented equivalents and may not clear at all.
  • Size distribution matters more than total count. A 2,000-piece lot of all-small sizes is worth less than 2,000 pieces spread across the curve — mid sizes (M/L) clear fastest for the most popular dog breeds. Ask for the size curve in units before pricing.
  • Breed-fit bias. Lots cut for one market's dominant breeds (bulldog-cut chest widths, long-back dachshund cuts) may fit poorly elsewhere; confirm the cut type for your destination channel.

Buyers who photograph the size chart alongside a tape-measured sample garment during inspection can list with confidence immediately — the highest-return QC habit in this category.

4. Compliance and materials

Pet apparel is not regulated like children's clothing, but retail channels apply textile rules that decide sellability:

  • Fabric chemistry. Azo-dye and formaldehyde restrictions apply to textiles in EU-bound channels; lots without supplier fabric declarations are usually restricted to less-regulated channels or deeply discounted.
  • Small parts. Buttons, bells, bows and decorative studs must survive a pull test — detachable small parts generate choking-hazard complaints and channel returns, especially on costume items.
  • Light-up costumes. LED-equipped costumes and collars contain batteries and follow UN38.3/MSDS-style lithium shipping documentation; check battery compartment corrosion on stored stock.
  • Performance claims. Reflective and waterproof claims should be supported by supplier documentation; unverified "waterproof" claims on aged PU stock are a common dispute source.

On branding: genuine OEM surplus of known pet-apparel brands follows the usual trademark-exhaustion logic — resale of genuine goods as-is is generally permitted, but re-labelling, logo stripping or re-branding is not. Licensed cartoon-character costumes (holiday sets printed with movie characters) are a recurring IP trap; undocumented character-print lots are best skipped entirely.

5. Grading and QC: closures, seams and storage odour

  • Hook-and-loop (Velcro) closures. Aged closures lose grip — the category's number-one hidden defect. Press-test and pull-test closures on sampled pieces; weak closures price the whole lot as B-grade.
  • Seam and harness-point pull tests. Stress seams and any harness attachment points by hand; dry-stored knits and cheap stitching give way under light force.
  • Storage odour. Open cartons and smell: storage odour and mildew on folded textiles kill retail value; odour at carton level, not piece level.
  • Elastic and ribbing aging. Stretched-out cuffs, waistbands and leg bands are common on long-stored stock; check recovery on samples.
  • PU coating flex test. Flex raincoats and waterproof coats by hand — hydrolysed coatings crack or delaminate visibly.
  • Filling clumping. Quilted winter jackets with clumped or shifted filling look second-hand; squeeze-test samples.
  • Set completeness. Costume sets (cape + hat + leg bands) must be counted complete; partial sets price like mixed grades.

6. How to buy: a five-step checklist

Step 1 — Fix the size documentation first

Before price, get the size chart with back length and chest girth per size, plus the size distribution in units. Pet apparel without measured size documentation is inventory, not merchandise.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • date of manufacture or season (textile age and season fit);
  • full carton photos including inner packing and size labels;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card.

Step 3 — Inspect above pallet volume

Third-party inspection in Yiwu, Jinhua or Qingdao typically costs USD 100–200 per man-day and routinely catches aged closures, odour and size-curve mismatches that photos never show. Bring a tape measure.

Step 4 — Buy on the season clock

Winter apparel prices deepest in February–April; holiday costumes deepest in November–December. Buying off-season trades deeper discounts against holding inventory until demand returns — plan the storage cost before the purchase.

Step 5 — Consolidate with the rest of the pet channel

Pet clothing is light and cube-efficient; it rides nearly free in containers already paying for toys, feeding products or household stock. Match apparel lots to other pet lots from the same belts for a near-free landed-cost lever.

Frequently asked questions

Why does pet clothing end up as stock lots?

Pet apparel is the most seasonal corner of pet goods. Winter coats turn surplus in February–April as chains clear cold-weather assortments, holiday costumes clear in November–December, subscription boxes rotate designs monthly, and cancelled exports, promotional tails and packaging refreshes add year-round flow. Cheap, light, large-run production means modest overproduction becomes big sellable lots.

What do pet clothing stock lots typically cost?

Indicative ranges commonly seen: USD 0.2–0.8/pc bandanas, USD 0.5–1.5/pc t-shirts and dresses, USD 1–3/pc sweaters, hoodies and raincoats, USD 2–6/pc insulated winter jackets, USD 0.5–3/pc holiday costumes. Ranges vary with quantity, grade and seasonality.

What is the sizing chaos trap in pet apparel?

There is no unified pet sizing standard — brands size by back length, chest girth, neck or weight, and the same letter size can differ by centimetres across makers. Lots without a measured size chart are hard to resell, and mixed-size lots without documented size distribution typically price 20–40% below documented equivalents. Demand a size chart with measurements per size and the size curve in units.

Do pet clothes need compliance documents?

Pet apparel is not children's clothing, but retailers apply textile standards: azo-dye and formaldehyde fabric restrictions matter for EU-bound lots; buttons, bells and bows should survive a pull test to avoid choking complaints; light-up costumes contain batteries and follow UN38.3/MSDS-style shipping rules; reflective and waterproof claims should be documented.

How should buyers grade pet apparel stock lots?

Hook-and-loop (Velcro) closure strength is the signature check — aged closures are the number-one hidden defect. Also pull-test seams and harness points, smell-check cartons for storage odour, inspect elastic and ribbing aging, flex-test PU coatings on raincoats, squeeze-test jacket filling, and count costume accessory sets complete.
