1. Where warehouse surplus comes from

China's supply chain for this category splits into two belts: plastic crates, bins and pallets from the plastic-injection belts (Taizhou in Zhejiang — widely regarded as China's plastic-mould production hub — plus Guangdong), and steel racking, trolleys and pallet trucks from Jiangsu and Shandong racking and material-handling bases. Surplus reaches the market through five doors:

  • E-commerce warehouse closures and downsizing. Fulfilment sites that close, merge or right-size liquidate whole floors: rows of matching racks, trolleys and crates in working condition, often at a fraction of new price. Uniformity is the feature — matched crates and racks resell faster than mixed lots.
  • Cancelled export orders. Finished crate, pallet or racking runs that lose their buyer mid-schedule price aggressively — factory-new, often still in shrink-wrap, and the best documentation carry-over on this market.
  • Fleet and equipment refreshes. Logistics companies and warehouses replace equipment on cycles (pallet trucks, trolleys, cage carts) and retire usable gear in bulk — a used-condition door that prices deepest but needs the most checking.
  • Packaging-spec changes. When a brand or retailer changes its crate or pallet specification (dimensions, weight rating, RFID pockets), the entire obsolete stock strands as new but dimension-superseded goods. These lots clear deepest — and are fine for any buyer whose own footprint doesn't need to match the old system.
  • Trade-fair and demonstration surplus. Racking and handling-equipment fair samples retire as single-model lots — new condition, sometimes with display wear.

3. The signature check: UV brittleness

Plastic crates and bins are the volume item in this category, and their hidden killer is UV aging:

  • Ask where it was stored. Outdoor or window-adjacent storage ages polypropylene and HDPE into brittle plastic even when the crate looks intact. Warehouse-stored lots avoid the worst of it — ask the question before anything else.
  • Read the surface. Chalky texture, faded or sun-bleached colour, and hairline cracks at corners and ribs are the visible tells of advanced aging. Colour mismatch inside one lot (older batches mixed with newer) usually means mixed storage histories.
  • Flex-test under load. Load sample crates to their claimed working weight and flex or drop-test from low height. Aged plastic cracks at rib roots and corner welds — a failure that photographs never show. Rejected crates from rental-pool fleets (returnable transport items) deserve exactly this test.
  • Price the storage history. A warehouse-stored, single-batch crate lot can trade near the top of its band; an outdoor-aged mixed-batch lot belongs at the bottom of the band or below — its remaining service life is short.

4. Stackability, dimensions and the mixed-lot trap

  • Footprints must match to stack. Crates stack reliably only when footprint dimensions and stacking features (rim profiles, lid types) match. Lots assembled from different orders or factories often combine near-identical but slightly different footprints — unstable stacks shed cartons in transit and force double-handling. Verify footprint match across the lot, or treat each model as its own stack.
  • Dimension-superseded lots are bargains with one condition. Spec-change residue is new goods at deep prices — the condition is that your operation doesn't need to inter-stack with the old dimension system. Confirm the exact external and internal dimensions in writing before committing.
  • Load ratings: verify, don't assume. Static and dynamic load ratings (crates typically claim in the tens of kg working; pallets in the 500–1,500 kg dynamic region depending on design) should be checkable on moulded-in markings or spec sheets. Unmarked lots price below marked ones because buyers can't verify claims.
  • Racking completeness mirrors the flat-pack trap. Steel shelving bays ship as kits: uprights, beams, shelf panels, bolts and safety pins. Missing components strand the goods the same way missing hardware bags strand flat-pack furniture — count components per bay against the packing list, and inspect beam-hook wear and upright bends on used racking.

5. Grade documentation and freight lines

  • Food-grade marks decide the channel. Crates and pallets destined for food, feed or pharma handling typically need a food-grade material declaration (virgin PP/HDPE meeting EU 1935/2004-style or FDA-style food-contact expectations — the same document line covered in the tableware guide). Recycled-material lots trade well into general cargo, industrial and agricultural channels but not food contact — the documentation gap prices as a discount.
  • Hydraulics follow the function-test rule. Hand pallet trucks live and die on hydraulic seals: lift a sample truck with a real load, hold it at height, watch for sink. Seal replacement rarely makes economic sense at clearance prices — a sinking truck prices as scrap-plus.
  • Casters and bearings are the trolley value decider. Spin and roll sample trolleys under load; check for flat-spotted wheels, seized swivel bearings and bent axles. Replacement casters are cheap relative to new trolleys but add labour cost per unit.
  • Freight math is volume-heavy. Crates and bins nest or stack well (ask for nested-carton or nested-pallet quantities — nesting ratios typically run 2:1 to 4:1 depending on design), but racking is long and awkward: uprights often exceed standard carton limits and price into oversized-freight territory. Compute freight per unit for your destination before negotiating, and see the mixed container guide for consolidation practice.
  • Trademark note. Branded crates and rental-pool fleet residue carry trademarks owned by their owners — resale is typically covered by trademark exhaustion only when goods entered the market legitimately; never re-label, and audit branded items before committing.

6. How to buy: five-step checklist

Step 1 — Identify the door

Cancelled exports and spec-change residue are factory-new and documentation-friendly; warehouse-closure and fleet-refresh lots are used-condition and need the most testing; fair samples need display-wear checks.

Step 2 — Demand three proofs

  • photos of moulded-in markings (load ratings, material codes, food-grade marks) and full-bay racking including components;
  • a live warehouse video with a handwritten date card, showing a crate flex-tested under load and a pallet truck lifting and holding;
  • exact external/internal dimensions per model in writing, and material declarations where food-grade claims are made.

Step 3 — Flex, lift and roll

Flex-test crates under load, lift-and-hold pallet trucks, roll trolleys and cycle casters — plastics and hydraulics fail in ways photos hide.

Step 4 — Verify stackability and completeness

Check footprint and stacking-profile match across crate models in the lot, count racking components per bay against the packing list, and confirm nesting ratios for shipping.

Step 5 — Compute freight per unit first

Get nested and stacked dimensions, compute CBM per unit for crates and oversized rates for racking, then negotiate on landed cost. Volume is the margin lever in this category.

What is the UV-brittleness problem in plastic crate lots?

Plastic crates and bins stored outdoors or near windows age through UV exposure: the plastic becomes brittle and crates that look fine crack when loaded or dropped. Check sample crates for chalky surfaces, faded colour and hairline cracks at corners and ribs, flex-test samples under load, and price outdoor-stored lots significantly below warehouse-stored lots because their remaining service life is short.

Why do mixed crate lots create a stackability problem?

Crates stack reliably only when footprint dimensions and stacking features match. Mixed lots from different orders or factories often combine near-identical but slightly different footprints, which produces unstable stacks that shed cartons in transit and force double-handling in the warehouse. Verify footprint and stacking-profile match across the lot — or treat each model as its own stack.

Do plastic pallets and crates need food-grade documentation?

Recycled-material crates trade well into general cargo and industrial channels but usually cannot enter food-contact handling — the missing grade documentation prices as a discount, and mixing grades inside one lot lowers the whole lot's channel.
